The Pullback Playbook

Six rules turn raw price into a trade: find the swing, draw the zone, enter at 50%, bracket it with a symmetric stop and target — then, at every trend change, relabel the old structure as a new zone. The twist this research found: the rule loses money exactly as designed, and wins once you take the other side of every signal.

Six mechanical steps

No indicators, no discretion. Every zone comes from the same swing detector; every entry, stop and target come from the shape of the zone itself.

1

Track the swing

No fixed lookback and no retracement threshold. A candidate high is simply the highest point since the last confirmed low — it keeps moving with every new high. It becomes a confirmed swing the instant a bar fails to extend it, and price starts building a candidate low instead. Same rule, mirrored, for lows.

2

Draw the zone from the pullback leg

Only the pullback leg becomes a tradeable zone while a trend holds — high-to-low in an uptrend, low-to-high in a downtrend. The leg that actually extends the trend is structure only: it sits dormant, untraded, until the trend inverts.

3

Mark the 50%

Every zone gets exactly one trigger: the midpoint between its top and bottom. Price doesn't need to fill the whole zone — a single wick tagging the 50% line is enough to arm the trade.

4

Set stop and target at the edges

Stop and target sit at the zone's opposite boundaries — built into its shape, no separate calculation. Under the rule as designed, that's a fixed 1:1 risk:reward on every single trade.

5

Flip zones at trend inversion

When a new low breaks the last confirmed low, the uptrend is over — and the leg that was structure only a moment ago (the last low up to the last high) becomes the first zone of the new downtrend. No new calculation, just a relabeling of the same leg.

6

Trade the fade

This is the part the backtests forced: instead of buying the low-side zone and selling the high-side zone the way the structure implies, take the opposite side of every signal. Same zone, same 50% entry, same stop and target shape — only the trade direction inverts.

Five trends, four flips

The same six steps, stretched across a longer run: thirty confirmed swings grouped into five trends of five to seven swings apiece, with the flip rule firing at every one of the four reversals. Why the flip

Backtested on full price history across four pairs, with a modeled 1.6-pip spread and no other costs. Same zones, same entries — only the traded side changes.

as designed trade the fade (inverted) dashed line = breakeven, profit factor 1.0

As designed, every pair loses over its full price history — profit factor between 0.52 and 0.66, worse than a coin flip after spread. Take the exact same zones and just trade the other side, and profit factor moves to between 1.17 and 1.42 on every pair. The edge sits entirely in pullback zones — flip zones stay flat-to-losing whichever side you trade them.

Research backtest, not a live-verified edge: EURUSD/GBPUSD/USDJPY/EURGBP, full available history, modeled 1.6-pip spread, no slippage, latency, or broker-specific fills. A small share of trades (under 3%) hit both stop and target within the same bar — an unresolvable OHLC limitation — which is why inverted win rates aren't exact mirror images of the originals.
